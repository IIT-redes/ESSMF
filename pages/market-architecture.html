The structure of exchange

Market architecture

The Entire Market Architecture pillar fixes the high-level structure of the market: the sub-markets that exist, their timing and type, the services and products exchanged, where procurement is located, and the roles of the actors involved.

Return to feedback section

Source-grounded tutorial

How to think about this pillar

The TMF treats market architecture as a system of interacting sub-markets rather than as one undifferentiated marketplace. The architecture pillar therefore answers the first design question: what are the exchange spaces that exist, and what must be specified for each of them before coordination and clearing can be analysed? OneNet D11.2 updates this pillar with more explicit timing, product and participant descriptors so that different demonstrators can be described with the same vocabulary.

OneNet D11.2 — updated TMF, Chapter 5.1Troncia et al. (2023) — TMF and OneNet lessons

Market-design intelligence

Read the pillar as a design procedure

Use this pillar first: it establishes the market-design objects that every later pillar must coordinate, clear, operate, and constrain. OneNet D11.2 treats it as the high-level description of the architecture as a whole and extends the earlier TMF with more explicit timing, product, and participant descriptors.

1. Establish the sub-market landscape

Identify how many sub-markets form the architecture and record the timing and market type of each one. D11.2 adds Gate Opening Time (GOT), Gate Closure Time (GCT) and Market Time Unit (MTU) to make the temporal dimension explicit.

  • Number of sub-markets.
  • GOT, GCT and MTU for each sub-market.
  • Sub-market type: bilateral negotiation, auction or exchange market.

2. Separate service from product

The TMF distinguishes the system need from the tradable unit used to procure flexibility. This separation is essential when one product can serve more than one system need or when similar services are procured through different products.

  • Service: the system need/action to be delivered.
  • Product: the tradable unit acquired from flexibility providers.
  • Type of product and technical requirements, added explicitly in D11.2.

3. Locate procurement in the grid

For each sub-market, describe the locational granularity, the responsible system operator and the voltage level of participating resources. The framework makes location part of market design rather than an implementation afterthought.

  • Area, feeder or node granularity at transmission or distribution level.
  • Responsible SO: TSO or DSO.
  • Voltage level of the resources.

4. Make actor roles and participation explicit

Record who buys, who sells and who operates the market, together with technology eligibility, aggregation and participation rules. D11.2 expands this part with allowed technologies, aggregation method and aggregation mix.

  • Buyer(s), seller(s) and market operator.
  • Allowed generation, load and storage technologies.
  • Aggregation method and aggregation mix.
  • Optional, compulsory or hybrid participation.

5. Use the pillar as a design-intelligence check

The OneNet analysis shows that temporal and spatial choices affect liquidity and competition, while product harmonisation can improve interoperability but may conflict with local technical needs. Actor boundaries must also be clear to avoid conflicts when products become interoperable across markets.

  • Ask whether the market window and procurement area are too narrow for liquidity.
  • Check whether product harmonisation improves interoperability without losing necessary local specificity.
  • Check that responsibilities remain unambiguous when the same resources can participate in several markets.

6. Continue through the TMF workflow

The 2023 paper applies the TMF bottom-up: identify buyer-seller interactions, define the sub-markets, describe each sub-market, describe interactions between sub-markets, then assemble the high-level market architecture. This pillar carries the first three steps.

Evidence from OneNet

Why these choices matter in real market design

The TMF does not treat these choices as independent checkboxes. The OneNet analysis highlights interactions among temporal scope, spatial scope, product harmonisation, liquidity, competition, and actor responsibilities.

Temporal and spatial scope shape liquidity

The OneNet analysis links larger market windows and broader procurement areas to a larger potential pool of FSPs, while stressing that no generally valid size exists; the design must fit the case.

Source: Troncia et al. (2023), §4.1

Product harmonisation creates a design trade-off

Standardised or harmonised products can improve interoperability, liquidity and competition, especially when the same product can serve several system needs. The source also warns that local technical needs may justify tailored features.

Source: Troncia et al. (2023), §4.1

Actor boundaries matter when markets become interoperable

When resources and products can move across markets, the architecture must make roles and responsibilities explicit to avoid inefficient or conflicting actions between system operators and market operators.

Source: Troncia et al. (2023), §4.1

Use the pillar bottom-up

The TMF application starts from buyer–seller interactions, defines sub-markets, describes each sub-market with the TMF features, then describes interactions and reconstructs the overall architecture.

Source: Troncia et al. (2023), §3

Standard terminology repository

Definitions used by the TMF

The definitions below preserve the terminology used by OneNet D11.2 and the Troncia et al. TMF paper. Where D11.2 extends the earlier framework, that update is stated explicitly.

Market architecture

The whole mechanism through which electricity products are exchanged to operate a delimited power system. In the TMF it is represented as interacting sub-markets.

Source: Troncia et al. (2023), §2

Sub-market

A market operated by one market operator that is responsible for clearing that specific market according to a specific objective.

Source: Troncia et al. (2023), §2

Gate Opening Time (GOT)

The TMF temporal sub-feature used to record when a sub-market gate opens. D11.2 adds GOT, together with GCT and MTU, to improve the description of the market architecture time dimension.

Source: OneNet D11.2, §5.1.1 / Fig. 5.2

Gate Closure Time (GCT)

The TMF temporal sub-feature used to record when a sub-market gate closes. In D11.2 it is a key compatibility variable when analysing how local and central markets can interact or forward bids.

Source: OneNet D11.2, Table 2.1

Market Time Unit (MTU)

The time granularity of the market interval/product recorded for a sub-market. D11.2 demonstrator comparisons use MTU values such as 15 minutes and 1 hour and identify MTU mismatches as a potential barrier to bid forwarding.

Source: OneNet D11.2, Table 2.1

Sub-market type

The market-based solution used to acquire flexibility, such as bilateral negotiation, auction or exchange market.

Source: Troncia et al. (2023), §2.1 / OneNet D11.2 Fig. 5.2

Service

The action generally undertaken by the system operator to mitigate a technical scarcity that would otherwise undermine network operation or create stability risks.

Source: Troncia et al. (2023), §2.1

Product

The tradable unit acquired by the network operator from flexibility providers that gives the option to deliver a service when activated.

Source: Troncia et al. (2023), §2.1

Type of product

An explicit D11.2 Product(s) sub-feature that records the form of the exchanged product. D11.2 derives its options from electricity-market product lists and uses it to compare compatibility across markets.

Source: OneNet D11.2, §5.1.1 / Table 2.1

Technical requirements

All technical properties that define the product based on the system service for which it is procured; D11.2 gives ramping as an example of such a property.

Source: OneNet D11.2, §5.1.1 / Table 2.1

Locational granularity

The level of detail used to represent the location of a buying or selling unit; TMF options include areas, feeders and nodes at transmission or distribution level.

Source: OneNet D11.2, Table 2.1 / Fig. 5.2

Responsible System Operator (SO)

The actor taking the system-operator role for the sub-market; the TMF records whether that responsibility lies with a TSO or DSO.

Source: Troncia et al. (2023), §2.1 / OneNet D11.2 Fig. 5.2

Market Operator (MO)

The actor responsible for operation/clearing of the sub-market. The TMF distinguishes TSO, DSO or an independent market operator as possible market-operator arrangements.

Source: Troncia et al. (2023), §2 / OneNet D11.2 Fig. 5.2

Flexibility/Service Provider (FSP/SP)

The selling side that provides eligible flexibility or system-service capability, individually or through aggregation.

Source: OneNet D11.2, Fig. 5.2 and demonstrator tables

Allowed technologies

The D11.2 market-participant sub-feature that states which resource classes are eligible; Figure 5.2 explicitly lists generators, loads and storage.

Source: OneNet D11.2, Table 2.1

Aggregation method

The D11.2 sub-feature that records how resources may be aggregated. Figure 5.2 lists none, area, voltage level, substation, other, and not applicable as high-level options.

Source: OneNet D11.2, Fig. 5.2

Aggregation mix allowed

The D11.2 sub-feature that records what types of resources/bids may be combined inside an aggregation; Figure 5.2 lists technology-based, bid-based and other as high-level options.

Source: OneNet D11.2, Fig. 5.2

Participation in sub-market

Optional means providers may choose to participate; compulsory means certain parties are obliged to participate under defined circumstances; hybrid combines compulsory and optional participation.

Source: Troncia et al. (2023), §2.1

Apply the pillar

Expert mapping checklist

Use these checks when you map an existing market or test whether the pillar can guide a new design.

  • Can every sub-market be placed unambiguously on a common time axis using GOT, GCT and MTU?
  • Can the system service be distinguished from the product used to procure it?
  • Can an external reviewer identify the buyer, seller, market operator, responsible SO and eligible technologies without relying on local institutional knowledge?
  • Is the spatial granularity explicit enough to compare the market with another jurisdiction?
  • Are aggregation and participation rules explicit enough to judge interoperability and bid-forwarding potential?
