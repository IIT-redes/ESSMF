The physics inside the market

Network representation

The Network Representation pillar specifies how network constraints are represented in the market architecture and at which market phase those constraints are introduced.

Return to feedback section

Source-grounded tutorial

How to think about this pillar

This pillar is the interface between market abstraction and physical feasibility. The earlier TMF paper calls it Grid Representation; D11.2 uses Network Representation and retains the two core questions: how network constraints are represented, and when they enter the market process. The choice determines data needs, computational burden, information exchange and the likelihood that a market-clearing result is physically feasible without later corrective action.

OneNet D11.2 — updated TMF, Chapter 5.1Troncia et al. (2023) — TMF and OneNet lessons

Market-design intelligence

Read the pillar as a design procedure

Use this pillar to make explicit how much of the physical network the market “sees” and when that information constrains procurement or activation.

1. Choose the representation of network constraints

The TMF distinguishes different levels of network representation. The 2023 paper explains comprehensive grid data and partial grid data; D11.2 also lists empirical rules and definition of procurement areas in the updated option set.

2. Understand the fidelity/data trade-off

Comprehensive grid data can support detailed power-flow calculations but increases data, computation and information-sharing requirements. Partial grid data can use sensitivities and constraint margins instead of a full network model. The framework makes this trade-off a market-design decision.

3. Decide when constraints enter the process

D11.2 lists timing options such as definition of procurement areas, prequalification, procurement, activation, measurement and settlement. The same architecture may therefore use network information at more than one stage.

4. Check the consequence for feasibility

A less comprehensive representation can reduce implementation burden but may require additional checks or corrective procedures after clearing. A more comprehensive representation can improve compliance with grid constraints but demands more data and computation.

5. Include governance and information-sharing implications

The OneNet analysis links network-model detail to grid-information availability, power-flow computation, data-exchange timing, cyber-security and privacy concerns. These implications should be reviewed together with the optimisation methodology rather than in isolation.

Evidence from OneNet

Why these choices matter in real market design

The OneNet/Troncia analysis frames network representation as a techno-economic trade-off between physical fidelity and the data, computation, information exchange and governance burden required to obtain it.

Comprehensive grid data increases physical detail and information burden

The peer-reviewed TMF explains comprehensive grid data as a representation of electrical grid properties that allows the optimisation to perform detailed power-flow calculations.

Source: Troncia et al. (2023), §2.5

Partial grid data can use sensitivities and margins

The TMF describes partial grid data as using the sensitivity of flexibility to critical voltage/current constraints and associated margins, avoiding a full network model in the market clearing.

Source: Troncia et al. (2023), §2.5

Less complete representation can require corrective checks

OneNet notes that representation comprehensiveness affects whether clearing results comply with grid constraints and therefore whether further corrective procedures are needed to ensure feasibility.

Source: Troncia et al. (2023), §4.5

The timing of network inclusion is itself a design choice

D11.2 allows network constraints to enter at procurement-area definition, prequalification, procurement, activation, measurement or settlement, meaning feasibility can be protected at several different points in the process.

Source: OneNet D11.2, Fig. 5.6

Information governance is part of the trade-off

The OneNet analysis connects grid-model detail with data availability, power-flow computation, data-exchange timing, cyber-security and privacy concerns among TSOs, DSOs and other stakeholders.

Source: Troncia et al. (2023), §4.5

Standard terminology repository

Definitions used by the TMF

OneNet D11.2 updates the option set of this pillar. Where the reports list an option without a stand-alone prose definition, the repository states that explicitly instead of inventing one.

Network / grid representation

The TMF pillar describing how network constraints are represented and when they are included in the market architecture. The 2023 paper uses “Grid Representation”; D11.2 uses “Network Representation”.

Source: Troncia et al. (2023), §2.5 / OneNet D11.2 §5.1.5

Representation of network constraints

The modelling approach used to account for physical network limits in the market design.

Source: Troncia et al. (2023), §2.5

Comprehensive grid data

A detailed representation of the electrical properties of the grid that allows the optimisation to calculate grid phenomena and perform full power-flow calculations.

Source: Troncia et al. (2023), §2.5

Partial grid data

A reduced representation using information such as flexibility sensitivities to critical voltage/current constraints and the corresponding margins, rather than a full grid model.

Source: Troncia et al. (2023), §2.5

Empirical rules

An explicit representation option listed in the updated D11.2 TMF. D11.2 lists the option but does not provide a separate prose definition in §5.1.5.

Source: OneNet D11.2, Fig. 5.6

Definition of procurement areas

An explicit D11.2 option that uses the delineation of procurement areas as part of how network needs/constraints are represented in the market architecture.

Source: OneNet D11.2, Fig. 5.6 and demonstrator tables

Timing of inclusion of network constraints

The market phase at which network constraints are introduced into the acquisition process.

Source: Troncia et al. (2023), §2.5 / OneNet D11.2 Fig. 5.6

Prequalification

A possible timing point at which technical eligibility and grid/product capability are checked before procurement.

Source: OneNet D11.2, Table 2.2 / Fig. 5.6

Procurement phase

The phase in which the buyer’s need meets sellers’ offers and binding product-exchange agreements are determined, including bid collection and market clearing.

Source: OneNet D11.2, Table 2.2

Activation

The process that triggers delivery of the procured service.

Source: OneNet D11.2, Table 2.2

Measurement

The process used to observe provider and/or grid behaviour during service provision and collect legally relevant measurements.

Source: OneNet D11.2, Table 2.2

Settlement

The procedures used to determine and execute the monetary exchange between buyer and seller based on measured service provision.

Source: OneNet D11.2, Table 2.2

Apply the pillar

Expert mapping checklist

Use these checks when you map an existing market or test whether the pillar can guide a new design.

  • Does the market use comprehensive grid data, partial grid data or empirical rules to protect network feasibility?
  • Can the data actually be shared at the required time and with acceptable computational burden?
  • At which market phases do network constraints influence eligibility, procurement, activation or verification?
  • If the representation is simplified, what additional check or corrective action ensures that the final result is physically feasible?
  • Is the network-representation choice consistent with the optimisation methodology and the information available to each system operator?
