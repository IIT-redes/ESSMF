Electricity System Services Market

A changing grid needs a compass for its markets.

A shared language for designing and comparing electricity-market frameworks across products, actors, timeframes, coordination layers, and network constraints. We warmly invite your expert feedback to sharpen the framework for research, regulation, and implementation.

What is the proposal?

A market framework is not a diagram. It is a grammar for change.

New flexibility markets are emerging around congestion management, voltage control, balancing, local system services, and distributed resource activation. Without a common structure, each market can become an isolated vocabulary. The Electricity System Services Market proposes a compact way to describe architecture, coordination, clearing, operation, and grid representation.

Its purpose is not to force every country or demonstrator into the same design. It is to make design choices explicit, comparable, and discussable.

Why does it matter?

Flexibility is a coordination problem before it becomes a market product.

Distribution-connected resources can support both local and system-wide needs. The same resource may be relevant for a DSO constraint, a TSO balancing need, a local congestion product, or an intraday position.

The critical design question is therefore not only what is the product, but how interacting markets avoid conflict and create value.

Worked implementation example

Spain case — from five filled TMF tables to one readable market architecture

The Spanish demonstrator illustrates the intended workflow of the framework: experts first fill the five pillar tables using a common vocabulary; those entries are then translated into a single market-architecture view. The figure is therefore a visual synthesis of the structured tables, not a separate design exercise.

How the filled tables are translated into the market-structure figure

D11.2 uses the TMF to describe the Spanish demonstrator systematically. The transformation can be read as a seven-step mapping from table entries to visual elements:

  1. 1. Create the market blocks from Pillar 1. The Spanish architecture table identifies four DSO-side sub-markets: LT-D-P-A, LT-D-P-AE, ST-D-P-E and RT-D-P-E. Each becomes a separate block in the DSO layer.
  2. 2. Place the blocks on the time axis using GOT, GCT and MTU. The long-term markets open months/weeks ahead; ST-D-P-E operates day-ahead or next-hour; RT-D-P-E is positioned on the day of delivery / near-real-time. Those rows determine the left-to-right placement of the blocks.
  3. 3. Place the blocks on the buyer/system-operator axis using the location and actor rows. The DSO is the buyer and responsible system operator; resources are connected at MV/LV and grouped in distribution-system areas. This is why the four OneNet sub-markets appear in the DSO layer rather than the TSO layer.
  4. 4. Interpret each block using service and product rows. All four local sub-markets support congestion management, while the traded attribute changes from active-power availability, to availability + activation, to activation. The OneNet harmonised-product mapping distinguishes predictive long-term, predictive short-term and corrective products.
  5. 5. Draw links using Pillar 2. The coordination table gives the DSO exclusive access to DERs in the demonstrator, defines conditional commitment from long-term availability markets into ST-D-P-E, and records the conceptual investigation of bid forwarding from ST-D-P-E toward the intraday energy market. These entries become the dashed participation-forwarding and solid bid-forwarding arrows in the figure.
  6. 6. Read the sequencing from Pillars 3 and 4. The Spanish design is decentralised, sequential and cost-minimising; the local markets use pay-as-bid auctions, discrete clearing, event-based DSO procurement, 10 kW minimum bids and simple bid structures. These choices explain why the figure shows separate market sessions rather than one simultaneously co-optimised clearing box.
  7. 7. Interpret the local nature through Pillar 5. Network constraints are represented with comprehensive/partial grid data and incorporated when procurement areas are defined and during procurement. This supports the local DSO procurement-area interpretation of the lower layer.
  8. Important boundary of the example: the filled Spanish TMF tables describe the OneNet local DSO markets. D11.2 then places existing day-ahead/intraday energy, balancing and TSO congestion-management markets above them to show the wider architecture. Their integration with the local short-term markets was an open task; the possible links were investigated conceptually rather than fully demonstrated.

The structure of exchange

Market architecture

Market architecture defines what is traded, where it is traded, which actors participate, and how individual sub-markets form one coordinated design space.

  • Number, type, and timing of sub-markets.
  • Services and products procured for system operation.
  • Locational scope, voltage level, and procurement area.
  • Roles of TSOs, DSOs, market operators, aggregators, flexibility providers, and platform operators.
Submit structured feedback
Expert review question

Does the Entire Market Architecture pillar provide a sufficiently complete and unambiguous common language to reconstruct an existing flexibility market and to specify a new distribution-level market before coordination and clearing are designed?

  • Can every sub-market be described consistently through number, GOT, GCT, MTU and sub-market type?
  • Are service and product sufficiently separated, including product type and technical requirements?
  • Are locational granularity, responsible SO, voltage level, buyer, seller, market operator, eligible technologies, aggregation and participation rules sufficient for cross-country comparison?
  • Which feature is missing, ambiguous, overlapping or too context-specific to support harmonised European market descriptions?
  • Would this pillar alone let another expert reproduce the high-level architecture of your market without relying on undocumented local knowledge?

The logic of interaction

Sub-market coordination

Sub-market coordination defines how flexibility is allocated when several markets, buyers, or system needs compete for the same distributed resources.

  • Allocation principles for flexibility across buyers and needs.
  • Coordination timing before, during, or after clearing.
  • Bid forwarding between related markets.
  • Avoiding double use, conflicting activation, and fragmented decisions.
Submit structured feedback
Expert review question

Does the Sub-market Coordination pillar capture the decisions needed to allocate the same flexibility resource pool across interacting local, TSO/DSO, wholesale or balancing sub-markets without double use, conflicting activation or avoidable barriers to value stacking?

  • For each pair of interacting sub-markets, are priority/exclusivity rules between system operators explicit enough?
  • Are TSO access to DERs and formal versus conditional commitment to bid selection sufficient to reconstruct how a real design works?
  • Is bid forwarding described at the right level, including whether filtering, aggregation or another intermediate processing step is required?
  • Are the coordination phases—prequalification, procurement, activation, measurement and settlement—sufficiently explicit?
  • Which additional rule would be needed to expose gaming risk, conflicting activations or inefficient overlapping procurement?

The clearing intelligence

Market optimization

Market optimization defines how bids, constraints, objectives, and sub-market interactions are transformed into a clearing result.

  • Optimization methodology and mathematical representation.
  • Separate, sequential, centralized, or simultaneous clearing.
  • Objective functions such as cost, welfare, security, or feasibility.
  • Use of OPF, simplified linear methods, sensitivities, or market rules.
Submit structured feedback
Expert review question

Do the TMF choices for optimisation methodology, the relationship among sub-market clearings and the clearing objective provide enough information to distinguish existing designs and to guide a new coordinated distribution-level market?

  • Is the distinction between centralised, decentralised and distributed organisation sufficiently clear for real implementations?
  • Does simultaneous, sequential or independent optimisation fully describe the relationship between coupled sub-markets in your case?
  • Is the objective—cost minimisation, social-welfare maximisation, reducing counter-activations or other—explicit enough to explain the resulting dispatch/procurement?
  • What additional information is needed to understand grid-data requirements, information exchange and computational responsibility?
  • Can the pillar reveal risks of double procurement, counter-activations or system-wide inefficiency created by local-first or sequential clearing?

The procedure of trust

Market operation

Market operation defines the practical rules that make the market usable: remuneration, clearing type, procurement frequency, bid properties, and market phases.

  • Remuneration scheme and product-attribute remuneration.
  • Market-clearing type and settlement logic.
  • Procurement frequency: long-term, day-ahead, intraday, or near-real-time.
  • Prequalification, baselining, activation, validation, and settlement.
Submit structured feedback
Expert review question

Does the Market Operation pillar contain the minimum operational information needed to compare how flexibility sub-markets actually run and to implement a new one without confusing operational rules with other TMF dimensions?

  • Are the remuneration options sufficient to describe how successful providers are paid in your market?
  • Is it clear whether availability, activation or both are remunerated and for active, reactive or apparent power?
  • Do continuous/discrete clearing and procurement frequency capture the temporal operating logic without duplicating GOT/GCT?
  • Are minimum bid size and bid structure sufficient to understand participation barriers and interoperability with other markets?
  • Which operational element, if any, is indispensable for implementation but genuinely missing from this pillar rather than belonging to another pillar/process?

The physics inside the market

Network representation

Network representation defines how physical constraints, topology, voltage, congestion, and location are included in the market design.

  • Representation of network constraints in or around clearing.
  • Timing of grid validation and constraint inclusion.
  • Use of zones, nodes, feeders, voltage levels, or virtual buses.
  • Sensitivity-based approximations for scalable local flexibility markets.
Submit structured feedback
Expert review question

Does the Network Representation pillar adequately capture how and when distribution/transmission constraints are introduced so that a market result can be physically feasible while remaining implementable in terms of data, computation and information sharing?

  • Is the distinction between comprehensive grid data, partial grid data and empirical rules sufficient for the network models used in practice?
  • At which phases should network constraints be introduced: procurement-area definition, prequalification, procurement, activation, measurement and/or settlement?
  • What validation or corrective step is required when simplified network representation is used?
  • Does the pillar expose the trade-off between physical fidelity and data/computational/cyber-security burden?
  • Which additional representation or timing option is necessary for real DSO-level implementation, if any?
