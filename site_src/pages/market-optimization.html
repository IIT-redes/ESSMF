The clearing intelligence

Market optimization

The Market Optimisation pillar describes how sub-markets are cleared, how clearing processes relate to each other, and what objective the optimisation pursues.

Source-grounded tutorial

How to think about this pillar

This pillar turns market architecture and coordination rules into a clearing logic. OneNet distinguishes who performs optimisation across network levels, whether coupled sub-markets are cleared together or in sequence, and which objective governs the outcome. These choices change information needs, implementation complexity and the risk of economically sub-optimal or conflicting results.

OneNet D11.2 — updated TMF, Chapter 5.1Troncia et al. (2023) — TMF and OneNet lessons

Market-design intelligence

Read the pillar as a design procedure

Read this pillar only after the architecture and coordination rules are clear. It explains how the market turns bids and constraints into a selected set of transactions.

1. Choose the optimisation methodology

Centralised optimisation uses one algorithm across voltage levels and system operators. Decentralised optimisation uses separate algorithms for different system levels and therefore requires coordination. Distributed organisation is defined in the TMF as having no externally driven optimisation.

2. Choose how coupled sub-markets are solved

Simultaneous optimisation solves the relevant sub-markets jointly while sharing resources. Sequential optimisation clears one before the other. Independent optimisation clears markets in parallel without sharing resources, although some clearing constraints may still be shared.

3. State the optimisation objective

The TMF includes objectives such as cost minimisation, social-welfare maximisation and reduction of counter-activations. The 2023 paper defines welfare maximisation through the combined producer/FSP and buyer/SO surplus and notes that other operational objectives may also be used.

4. Analyse the information and coordination burden

OneNet links centralised designs to stronger grid representation and network-information exchange requirements. Decentralised/sequential arrangements may lower those requirements but can produce a sub-optimal result from the perspective of the overall system.

5. Check for double procurement and counter-actions

The optimisation strategy influences whether one market action creates an expensive countermeasure elsewhere and how double procurement is prevented. OneNet’s demonstrators use different solutions: common centralised clearing versus sequential local-first clearing with aggregation/forwarding rules.

Evidence from OneNet

Why these choices matter in real market design

OneNet links optimisation choices to information-sharing requirements, implementation complexity, overall efficiency and the risk of counter-actions or double procurement.

Centralised and decentralised designs solve different coordination problems

Centralised optimisation can embed TSO–DSO coordination inside one clearing problem but increases grid-data and information-sharing requirements. Decentralised designs use separate optimisation problems and therefore need explicit coordination rules.

Source: Troncia et al. (2023), §2.3 and §4.3

Sequence changes economic outcomes

Sequential local-first clearing can protect local constraints and reduce information exchange, but the OneNet analysis notes that it may lead to a sub-optimal result from the perspective of the overall power system.

Source: Troncia et al. (2023), §4.3

The strategy must address counter-actions and double procurement

If one market action creates a need for an expensive countermeasure elsewhere, the clearing architecture is not neutral. OneNet uses optimisation strategy and coordination rules to expose and mitigate this issue.

Source: Troncia et al. (2023), §4.3

Objective functions must be explicit

The TMF distinguishes cost minimisation, social-welfare maximisation and reduction of counter-activations because two architectures with similar timing and products can behave differently if they optimise different objectives.

Source: OneNet D11.2, Fig. 5.4; Troncia et al. (2023), §2.3

Standard terminology repository

Definitions used by the TMF

The repository below uses the optimisation terminology and option set formalised in the TMF and retained in OneNet D11.2.

Market optimisation methodology

The organisational approach used to optimise across related sub-markets and system levels. TMF options are centralised, decentralised and distributed.

Source: Troncia et al. (2023), §2.3 / OneNet D11.2 Fig. 5.4

Centralised optimisation

One algorithm considers the relevant voltage levels and solves the system needs of all participating system operators in one optimisation.

Source: Troncia et al. (2023), §2.3

Decentralised optimisation

Separate algorithms optimise different system levels, with at least one optimisation for transmission and one for distribution; coordination is therefore required between optimisation levels.

Source: Troncia et al. (2023), §2.3

Distributed organisation

The TMF option in which there is no externally driven optimisation.

Source: Troncia et al. (2023), §2.3

Sub-market optimisation strategy

The rule describing how a pair of sub-markets is optimised relative to each other. TMF options are simultaneous, sequential and independent.

Source: Troncia et al. (2023), §2.3

Simultaneous optimisation

The relevant sub-markets are optimised at the same time while sharing resources.

Source: Troncia et al. (2023), §2.3

Sequential optimisation

One sub-market is optimised before the other.

Source: Troncia et al. (2023), §2.3

Independent optimisation

Markets are cleared in parallel rather than jointly and do not share resources/bids, although clearing constraints may be shared when needed.

Source: Troncia et al. (2023), §2.3

Cost minimisation

A TMF clearing-objective option in which the optimisation seeks the least-cost procurement outcome under the modelled requirements and constraints.

Source: OneNet D11.2, Fig. 5.4

Social-welfare maximisation

An objective that maximises the combined surplus of the producer/FSP side and the buyer/SO side.

Source: Troncia et al. (2023), §2.3

Reduction of counter-activations

A TMF objective aimed at limiting actions in one market that must be offset by opposite or corrective actions in another market.

Source: Troncia et al. (2023), §2.3 / OneNet D11.2 Fig. 5.4

Apply the pillar

Expert mapping checklist

Use these checks when you map an existing market or test whether the pillar can guide a new design.

  • Is it possible to identify whether the clearing is centralised, decentralised or distributed?
  • For every coupled pair of sub-markets, is the relationship simultaneous, sequential or independent?
  • Is the optimisation objective explicit and distinguishable from technical feasibility constraints?
  • Does the chosen architecture require grid/network information that the involved actors can realistically exchange?
  • How are counter-activations, double procurement and system-wide inefficiency detected or prevented?
