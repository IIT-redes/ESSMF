The logic of interaction

Sub-market coordination

The Sub-market Coordination pillar describes how sub-markets interact when multiple system operators or markets seek flexibility from the same resource pool, and in which market phase that coordination occurs.

Return to feedback section

Source-grounded tutorial

How to think about this pillar

This pillar is about allocation before it is about algorithms. It makes explicit which buyer has priority or exclusivity, whether selection is binding or conditional, whether unused bids may move to another market, and when coordination is performed. The OneNet analysis uses these choices to expose potential overlaps, double use, gaming opportunities and barriers to value stacking.

OneNet D11.2 — updated TMF, Chapter 5.1Troncia et al. (2023) — TMF and OneNet lessons

Market-design intelligence

Read the pillar as a design procedure

Apply this pillar to pairs of sub-markets that can draw on the same flexibility. The purpose is to make the allocation logic visible before optimisation choices are assessed.

1. Identify the pair of interacting sub-markets

The allocation principle is not one global rule for the whole architecture; it forms a link between two sub-markets. The TMF applies the allocation logic when markets overlap in time and seek flexibility from the same transmission/distribution resource pool under different system operators.

2. Set system-operator order

The TMF distinguishes priority for TSO, priority for DSO, exclusivity for TSO, exclusivity for DSO, and no priority nor exclusivity. These options determine who can access scarce flexibility first or whether access is shared.

3. Define commitment and access

A formal commitment makes the buyer’s bid selection binding within the stated coordination rules. Conditional commitment allows a first-stage local result to remain provisional until a later coordinated optimisation determines the final accepted bids. TSO access to DERs is recorded separately.

4. Decide whether bids can be forwarded

Bid forwarding transfers eligible bids that were not used in one market to another, potentially through an intermediate processing stage. OneNet uses it as a coordination channel for resource allocation and value stacking, but highlights the need to avoid conflicting activations and gaming.

5. Place coordination in the market process

The timeframe-for-coordination feature records the market phase in which coordination occurs. D11.2 lists prequalification, procurement, activation, measurement and settlement. Shared phases can reduce duplicated procedures, while some sub-markets may still require dedicated processes.

6. Use OneNet’s design tension as the review lens

The framework asks whether coordination improves overall resource allocation without creating conflictive overlap, artificial congestion or unnecessary implementation complexity. Multi-level designs can be simpler and more local; common markets can pursue broader economic efficiency but demand stronger coordination and information exchange.

Evidence from OneNet

Why these choices matter in real market design

OneNet uses this pillar to reveal where shared resources, overlapping markets and sequencing rules can create either value stacking or conflicts.

The allocation principle is pairwise, not a single market-wide label

The TMF treats the allocation principle as a link between two sub-markets, especially when different system operators procure flexibility in overlapping time windows from the same pool of resources.

Source: Troncia et al. (2023), §2.2

Bid forwarding can improve allocation and value stacking

OneNet treats forwarding of unused eligible bids as a coordination mechanism between markets, but it requires compatibility checks and may need intermediate processing such as filtering or aggregation.

Source: OneNet D11.2, §2.2 and §5.2

Coordination must also prevent gaming and conflicting activation

The source highlights conflictive overlap, artificial congestion, double use and incompatible activations as risks when the same resources are available to multiple markets.

Source: Troncia et al. (2023), §4.2

Harmonising market phases can reduce barriers

Coordinating shared processes such as prequalification can reduce duplicated procedures and participation barriers, although dedicated procedures may still be needed for market-specific requirements.

Source: Troncia et al. (2023), §4.2; OneNet D11.2 harmonisation recommendations

Standard terminology repository

Definitions used by the TMF

These terms describe the rules connecting sub-markets. They should be read as pairwise coordination descriptors rather than as isolated characteristics of one market.

Allocation principle of flexibility

The rule determining how available flexibility at transmission or distribution level is divided between system operators and sub-markets.

Source: Troncia et al. (2023), §2.2

Priority for the TSO

The TSO chooses flexibility first; bids not selected by the priority market may still be available to another sub-market when forwarding is allowed.

Source: Troncia et al. (2023), §2.2

Priority for the DSO

The DSO chooses distribution-connected flexibility first; the framework associates this option with formal commitment and possible bid forwarding.

Source: Troncia et al. (2023), §2.2

Exclusivity for the TSO

Only the TSO has access to the relevant bids; rejected bids are not forwarded under this option.

Source: Troncia et al. (2023), §2.2

Exclusivity for the DSO

Only the DSO has access to the relevant bids; the framework associates the option with formal commitment and no forwarding of rejected bids.

Source: Troncia et al. (2023), §2.2

No priority nor exclusivity

Neither SO is pre-prioritised. The TMF links this to conditional commitment in decentralised optimisation and formal commitment in centralised optimisation.

Source: Troncia et al. (2023), §2.2

Formal commitment to bid selection

The buyer’s selection is binding within the coordination arrangement even if another use of the same bid might be preferable from a broader welfare perspective.

Source: Troncia et al. (2023), §2.2

Conditional commitment to bid selection

A first market result remains provisional and is integrated into a later coordinated optimisation, which determines the final accepted bids and the beneficiary SO.

Source: Troncia et al. (2023), §2.2

TSO access to DERs

A TMF sub-feature recording whether the TSO can access flexibility from distributed energy resources within the coordination arrangement.

Source: OneNet D11.2, Fig. 5.3

Bid forwarding

Transfer of eligible bids not used in one market to another market, potentially through an intermediary processing step managed by a designated agent.

Source: Troncia et al. (2023), §2.2 / OneNet D11.2 §2.2

Timeframe for coordination

The market phase in which coordination is carried out between sub-markets.

Source: Troncia et al. (2023), §2.2

Market phase for coordination

D11.2 options include prequalification, procurement, activation, measurement and settlement.

Source: OneNet D11.2, Fig. 5.3

Apply the pillar

Expert mapping checklist

Use these checks when you map an existing market or test whether the pillar can guide a new design.

  • For each pair of interacting sub-markets, is it clear whether one SO has priority, exclusivity, or neither?
  • Is bid selection formal or conditional, and at what point does a provisional decision become binding?
  • Can unused eligible bids move to another market, and what filtering/aggregation step is required before forwarding?
  • At which market phases is coordination performed, and are any processes duplicated unnecessarily?
  • Could the chosen sequence create double use, conflicting activation, artificial congestion or gaming incentives?
