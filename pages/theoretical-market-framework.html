What is the idea?

A shared grammar for market design choices.

Why does it matter?

Distributed flexibility creates value only when markets and networks coordinate.

Who needs a common language?

Operators, regulators, market parties, active customers, and platform designers.

Where can it apply?

Local flexibility, harmonised products, market phases, and dashboard interfaces.

What is the proposal?

A market framework is not a diagram. It is a grammar for change.

Flexibility markets differ in services, products, actors, timing, and grid needs. This framework gives them a common language across architecture, coordination, clearing, operation, and network representation.

The goal is not one market model, but transparent and comparable design choices.

Architecture shapes the market
Physics enters the market

Why does it matter?

Flexibility is a coordination problem before it becomes a market product.

The same distributed resource can serve local DSO needs and wider system markets.

The key question is how interacting markets share flexibility without conflict.

Who needs a common language?

The framework connects actors who often see only one part of the market

TSOsSystem security, balancing, congestion, and system-wide coordination.
DSOsLocal constraints, voltage control, hosting capacity, and local activation.
Market operatorsClearing logic, transparency, settlement, and data exchange.
Aggregators & FSPsParticipation rules, bid properties, remuneration, and revenue stacking.
RegulatorsRoles, responsibilities, harmonisation, fairness, and implementation pathways.
Platform designersDashboards, interfaces, interoperability, and auditability.

Illustrative case

Case example — from five filled Theoretical Market Framework tables to one market-architecture view

A worked case shows the process: complete the five pillar tables with consistent market labels, then translate them into one architecture view.

How to fill the tables and translate them into the figure

Keep the same Market 1–4 labels across all five tables. Record only known information; use Additional Information for assumptions, exceptions, and pairwise conditions.

  1. 1. Pillar 1 defines each market: timing, product, location, and actors. Each market becomes a block.
  2. 2. Keep the same labels in Pillars 2–5 to describe coordination, clearing, operation, and network constraints.
  3. 3. Use “Not applicable” where needed and record uncertainty in Additional Information.
  4. 4. Position blocks in time using GOT/GCT/MTU and by layer using buyer, operator, and location.
  5. 5. Draw links only when coordination rules support them; use the remaining pillars to explain how the sessions work.
